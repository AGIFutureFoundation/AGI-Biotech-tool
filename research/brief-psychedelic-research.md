## Scope and legal notice — read first

Psilocybin, psilocyn, MDMA (midomafetamine), and LSD are **Schedule I controlled substances under U.S. federal law** (Controlled Substances Act, 21 U.S.C. § 812; psilocybin and psilocyn listed at 21 CFR 1308.11(d)). Federal law treats them as having high abuse potential, no currently accepted medical use, and a lack of accepted safety for use under medical supervision.

This brief covers **research evidence, therapy-model design, and the reimbursement landscape only**. It is **not an offer of treatment**, not medical advice, and not a claim that any psychedelic treats, cures, or diagnoses any condition. **No compound in this brief is proposed for human use** except through the regulatory paths stated in Section 2 (FDA-authorized clinical trials under an IND with DEA registration, or a state-regulated service model where one exists — and state models do not change federal status). Nothing here is a cure, treatment, diagnosis, or medical-device claim.

**Filed fact vs. proposed plan:** everything in Section 1 is published trial evidence as cited; everything in Section 6 is a proposed research agenda that does not yet exist. Do not mix the two.

## 1. What the literature actually supports — trial by trial

Reading rule used throughout: a **completed, peer-reviewed randomized trial** is stronger evidence than a **company press release**; a **between-group difference on a validated scale** is the result; a **response/remission percentage in one arm** is not. Where a result below comes only from a press release, that is stated. Where an effect is large in a small or waitlist-controlled study, that limitation is stated next to the number.

### 1.1 Psilocybin for depression

**Davis et al. 2020 — Johns Hopkins, major depressive disorder (COMPLETED, peer-reviewed).**
Randomized, waiting-list–controlled trial at Johns Hopkins Bayview; 27 randomized, 24 completed; two psilocybin sessions (20 mg/70 kg then 30 mg/70 kg) with ~11 hours of supportive psychotherapy. Published *JAMA Psychiatry*, 4 Nov 2020. At week 4, 71% of participants showed ≥50% reduction in depression symptoms (GRID-HAMD) and 54% were in remission. Between-group effect sizes vs. the waitlist were large (Cohen *d* ≈ 2.5–2.6). Limitations that matter: n = 24 completers, waitlist (not placebo) control, 4-week follow-up in the primary report, and expectancy effects cannot be excluded — participants knew they would receive psilocybin.
Source: https://hub.jhu.edu/2020/11/11/psilocybin-rapidly-treats-depression/ (as of 2026-11-07)

**Carhart-Harris et al. 2021 — Imperial College London, psilocybin vs. escitalopram (COMPLETED, peer-reviewed).**
Phase 2, double-blind, randomized, controlled trial, n = 59 with long-standing moderate-to-severe major depressive disorder; two 25 mg psilocybin doses + daily placebo vs. two 1 mg doses + 6 weeks of escitalopram, psychological support in both arms. Published *N Engl J Med* 2021;384:1402–1411. **Primary outcome (QIDS-SR-16 change at week 6) did not differ significantly**: −8.0 vs. −6.0 points, difference 2.0 (95% CI −5.0 to 0.9; p = 0.17). Secondary outcomes generally favored psilocybin — response 70% vs. 48%, remission 57% vs. 28% — but were **not corrected for multiple comparisons**. The authors' conclusion: larger, longer trials are required to compare psilocybin with established antidepressants. This trial does **not** license the claim that psilocybin is "better than SSRIs."
Source: https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/33852780/ (as of 2026-11-07)

**Goodwin et al. 2022 — COMPASS Pathways COMP001, Phase 2b, treatment-resistant depression (COMPLETED, peer-reviewed).**
Randomized, double-blind, dose-ranging trial, n = 233 with treatment-resistant depression, across 22 sites in 10 countries; single dose of COMP360 (synthetic psilocybin) 25 mg (n = 79), 10 mg (n = 75), or 1 mg control (n = 79), with psychological support. Published *N Engl J Med* 2022;387:1637–1648. Primary endpoint (MADRS change, baseline to week 3): −12.0 (25 mg), −7.9 (10 mg), −5.4 (1 mg). **25 mg vs. 1 mg difference: −6.6 points (95% CI −10.2 to −2.9; p < 0.001)**; 10 mg vs. 1 mg was not significant (−2.5; p = 0.18). Remission at week 3: 29.1% (25 mg) vs. 7.6% (1 mg). Sustained response at week 12 was **not** observed. Adverse events occurred in 77% of participants (headache, nausea, dizziness most common); suicidal ideation, suicidal behavior, or intentional self-injury occurred across all dose groups. This is the largest published psilocybin RCT in treatment-resistant depression and the basis for the Phase 3 program below.
Source: https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/36322843/ (as of 2026-11-07)

**COMPASS Pathways COMP005 — Phase 3, treatment-resistant depression (COMPLETED primary endpoint; PRESS RELEASE ONLY, not peer-reviewed as of early 2026).**
Randomized, double-blind, placebo-controlled; 258 participants across 32 U.S. sites; single 25 mg COMP360 dose vs. placebo. Announced 23 June 2025: **MADRS mean difference −3.6 points at week 6 (95% CI −5.7 to −1.5; p < 0.001)**; 25% of the 25 mg arm achieved a ≥25% MADRS reduction (a lower bar than the standard ≥50% response criterion). The independent Data Safety Monitoring Board found no unexpected safety findings and no clinically meaningful imbalance in suicidality between arms. Note the scale: MADRS runs 0–60, so a 3.6-point drug–placebo separation is statistically unambiguous and **clinically small** — whether patients and prescribers would notice the difference is the open question for FDA review. The FDA had not reviewed the data at announcement.
Source (company press release via BioSpace, verified by Firecrawl scrape 2026-11-07): https://www.biospace.com/press-releases/compass-pathways-successfully-achieves-primary-endpoint-in-first-phase-3-trial-evaluating-comp360-psilocybin-for-treatment-resistant-depression

**COMPASS Pathways COMP006 — Phase 3, treatment-resistant depression (COMPLETED primary endpoint; PRESS RELEASE ONLY).**
581 participants; two fixed 25 mg doses three weeks apart vs. 1 mg control. Announced 17 February 2026: **MADRS mean difference −3.8 points at week 6 (95% CI −5.8 to −1.8; p < 0.001)**. 26-week follow-up data (Part B, reported July 2026): 39% of the 25 mg arm had a clinically meaningful response at week 6 and maintained it on average through week 26 — but that responder-maintenance analysis is **post hoc**, and 58% of the arm had been retreated after week 9, so durability is a provisional signal, not a proven fact. Open-label 52-week data (COMP005 Part C, September 2026) reported an average ~13-point MADRS reduction from baseline at week 52 in participants who received an additional dose — open-label, uncontrolled, press-release data. COMPASS reported a rolling NDA submission on track for completion in Q4 2026, with a Commissioner's National Priority Voucher (CNPV) that could shorten FDA review; the company itself cautioned the voucher may not result in faster review or approval.
Source: https://markets.financialcontent.com/prnews.pressre/article/bizwire-2026-2-17-compass-pathways-successfully-achieves-primary-endpoint-in-second-phase-3-trial-evaluating-comp360-psilocybin-for-treatment-resistant-depression (as of 2026-11-07)

**What the psilocybin-depression record supports, honestly stated:** the peer-reviewed evidence shows a real, rapid signal in small-to-moderate trials, with the largest published RCT (Goodwin 2022) showing a −6.6-point MADRS separation at 3 weeks that did not sustain at 12 weeks. The two Phase 3 trials both met their primary endpoints, but with smaller separations (−3.6 and −3.8 MADRS points) than the Phase 2b result, and only in press releases so far. No psilocybin product is FDA-approved for depression as of 2026-11-07.

### 1.2 MDMA-assisted therapy for PTSD

**Mitchell et al. 2021 — MAPP1, Phase 3 (COMPLETED, peer-reviewed).**
Randomized, double-blind, placebo-controlled; n = 90 with severe PTSD; three MDMA sessions (80–120 mg with optional booster) plus structured preparation and integration psychotherapy vs. inactive placebo + the same psychotherapy. Published *Nature Medicine* 2021. Primary outcome CAPS-5 (Clinician-Administered PTSD Scale): mean reduction 24.4 points (MDMA) vs. 13.9 (placebo); **between-group effect size d ≈ 0.91**. ~67% of the MDMA group no longer met PTSD diagnostic criteria vs. ~32% on placebo.
Source: https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/33972795/ (as of 2026-11-07)

**Mitchell et al. 2023 — MAPP2, Phase 3 (COMPLETED, peer-reviewed).**
Confirmatory randomized Phase 3; n = 104 with moderate-to-severe PTSD; same protocol structure. Published *Nature Medicine* 2023. **Effect size d ≈ 1.0 on CAPS-5**; mean CAPS-5 reduction 23.7 (MDMA) vs. 14.8 (placebo); 71.2% met the response criterion (≥10-point CAPS-5 reduction) vs. 47.6% on placebo; ~71% no longer met PTSD criteria vs. ~48% on placebo. Together MAPP1 + MAPP2 are the largest published RCT evidence base for any psychedelic-assisted therapy, with effect sizes substantially larger than typical PTSD pharmacotherapy (SSRIs, d ≈ 0.3–0.5).
Source: https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/37709999/ (as of 2026-11-07)

**Regulatory outcome (see Section 2):** despite these two positive Phase 3 trials, the FDA issued a Complete Response Letter in August 2024 declining approval of Lykos Therapeutics' application. The trials' results and the application's rejection are both facts; the rejection was about the evidence package (blinding, safety-data capture, durability), not a finding that MDMA-assisted therapy "does not work."

### 1.3 LSD (lysergide) for anxiety and depression

**Robison et al. 2025 — MindMed MM120 Phase 2b, generalized anxiety disorder (COMPLETED, peer-reviewed in *JAMA*).**
Phase 2b, multicenter, randomized, double-blind, placebo-controlled dose-finding study; n = 198 adults with moderate-to-severe generalized anxiety disorder (HAM-A ≥ 20), 22 U.S. sites; single dose of MM120 (lysergide D-tartrate) 25, 50, 100, or 200 µg vs. placebo, in a monitored clinical setting **with no additional therapeutic intervention**. Primary outcome (HAM-A change at week 4): 100 µg vs. placebo least-squares mean difference −5.0 points (95% CI −9.6 to −0.4); 200 µg −6.0. At week 12, the 100 µg arm showed a **−7.7-point HAM-A difference vs. placebo (Cohen's d = 0.81)**; clinical response 65% and remission 48%. Most common adverse events were visual perceptual changes (92.5% at 100 µg vs. 10.3% placebo), nausea (40.0%), and headache (35.0%). FDA granted MM120 Breakthrough Therapy designation for generalized anxiety disorder in March 2024.
Source: https://www.researchgate.net/publication/395271281_Single_Treatment_With_MM120_Lysergide_in_Generalized_Anxiety_Disorder_A_Randomized_Clinical_Trial (as of 2026-11-07); designation date corroborated at https://www.lexblog.com/2026/10/26/tell-me-what-you-want-what-you-really-really-want-fdas-top-officials-put-their-psychedelics-position-in-writing/

**MindMed / Definium Therapeutics DT120 (MM120) Phase 3 — ONGOING at announcement, topline results reported 2026 (PRESS RELEASES ONLY, not peer-reviewed as of 2026-11-07).**
Three pivotal Phase 3 trials of an orally disintegrating tablet, notably **without co-occurring psychotherapy** (a different model from the psilocybin and MDMA programs): Voyage (GAD, n = 214) — topline August 2026, HAM-A difference −5.4 points vs. placebo at week 12 (p < 0.0001); Panorama (GAD, n = 245) — topline September 2026, HAM-A reductions 9.8 vs. 4.7 points; Emerge (major depressive disorder, n = 149) — topline June 2026, MADRS difference −8.1 points vs. placebo at week 6 (p < 0.0001). The company (renamed Definium Therapeutics, January 2026) reported plans to meet with the FDA before end of 2026 and apply for approval in the first half of 2027. **All three Phase 3 figures are company topline press releases; no peer-reviewed publication had appeared as of 2026-11-07. Treat them as unverified until published.**
Sources: https://cnspulse.com/compound/mm120/ and https://innerweave.substack.com/p/one-dose-a-checklist-and-a-ride-home (as of 2026-11-07)

### 1.4 Context: what IS approved — esketamine

Esketamine (Spravato, Janssen) is the only psychedelic-adjacent medicine FDA-approved for depression: nasal spray approved **5 March 2019** for treatment-resistant depression in adults (with an oral antidepressant), expanded August 2020 to major depressive disorder with acute suicidal ideation or behavior, and January 2025 as monotherapy for treatment-resistant depression. It is a **Schedule III** controlled substance, available only in REMS-certified settings. Esketamine is the S-enantiomer of ketamine — a dissociative anesthetic, not a classic psychedelic — and its approval does not imply anything about psilocybin, MDMA, or LSD.
Source: https://www.DRUGS.com/newdrugs/fda-approves-spravato-esketamine-nasal-resistant-depression-4926.html (as of 2026-11-07)

## 2. Regulatory status — exactly right

### 2.1 Federal: Schedule I, no classic psychedelic approved

- **Psilocybin, psilocyn, MDMA, and LSD are Schedule I** under the Controlled Substances Act. No classic psychedelic is FDA-approved for any indication as of 2026-11-07.
- Legal human research requires an FDA-authorized Investigational New Drug (IND) application and DEA Schedule I researcher registration, plus institutional review and controlled-substance handling. That is the only federal path to administering these compounds to humans outside a state service model (below).

### 2.2 MDMA-assisted therapy: Complete Response Letter, August 2024 — NOT approved

On **8 August 2024**, the FDA issued a **Complete Response Letter (CRL)** for NDA 215455 (midomafetamine capsules, Lykos Therapeutics) for PTSD. Verified directly from the FDA's published letter (Firecrawl scrape of the openFDA PDF, 2026-11-07): *"We have completed our review of this application, as amended, and have determined that we cannot approve this application in its present form"*; *"your application does not provide substantial evidence of effectiveness or establish the safety of your product to support the approval of midomafetamine for the treatment of [PTSD]."*

The deficiencies the FDA cited included, in the letter's own terms:
1. **Safety-data reliability** — the sponsor's training materials and safety manual defined an adverse event as excluding "positive" or "favorable" effects, inconsistent with the MAPP2 protocol; the FDA found the studies therefore failed to adequately characterize safety, acute effects, duration of impairment, or abuse-potential signals.
2. **Durability not demonstrated** beyond the 18-week end-of-study period.
3. A recommendation for **a new randomized trial with blinded long-term follow-up**, plus an independent audit of study records (per sponsor and press reporting of the letter's contents; the CRL itself is public at the URL below).

Context: the FDA's Psychedelic Drugs Advisory Committee had voted 9–2 against efficacy and unanimously on safety concerns at its June 4–5, 2024 meeting (reported; the CRL itself does not rest on the vote). Lykos laid off ~75% of its workforce and its CEO stepped down after the CRL. In August 2026 it was **reported** (trade press, mdmatherapy.news) that Resilient Pharmaceuticals — which acquired Lykos's MDMA assets — resubmitted the application **without** conducting the new Phase 3 trial the FDA requested. That resubmission is **unverified against any FDA primary source** as of 2026-11-07; there is no approved MDMA-assisted therapy in the United States and no approved timeline for one.

A CRL is not a prohibition and not a finding that the treatment does not work. It is a statement that the application, as filed, cannot be approved, plus what would need to change.

Source: https://download.open.fda.gov/crl/CRL_NDA215455_20240808.pdf (FDA CRL, verified by Firecrawl scrape 2026-11-07); https://www.mdmatherapy.news/mdma-fda-application-timeline (as of 2026-11-07)

### 2.3 Psilocybin: Breakthrough Therapy designation — NOT approval

**Breakthrough Therapy designation** is an FDA program for drugs treating a serious or life-threatening condition where *preliminary clinical evidence indicates the drug may demonstrate substantial improvement on a clinically significant endpoint over available therapies*. It is intended to **expedite development and review** — more FDA interaction, rolling review eligibility, organizational commitment. **It is not approval, and it does not mean the drug is effective or safe.** Roughly one in three designated drugs historically reaches approval (reported, New Atlas 2019).

Designations in this field, verified:
- **MDMA-assisted therapy for PTSD** — first psychedelic Breakthrough Therapy designation, **August 2017** (MAPS/Lykos). It later received the 2024 CRL above. Designation did not prevent rejection.
- **COMP360 (COMPASS Pathways) for treatment-resistant depression** — **October 2018**.
- **Usona Institute psilocybin for major depressive disorder** — **November 2019**.
- **MM120 (lysergide) for generalized anxiety disorder** — **March 2024**; also designated for major depressive disorder (reported 2026).
- **CYB003 (Cybin, deuterated psilocybin analog) for adjunctive major depressive disorder** — **March 2024** (reported).

Sources: https://www.americanbar.org/groups/health_law/resources/esource/2023-december/rescheduling-psilocybin/ ; https://www.lexblog.com/2026/10/26/tell-me-what-you-want-what-you-really-really-want-fdas-top-officials-put-their-psychedelics-position-in-writing/ (as of 2026-11-07)

**FDA guidance:** the FDA published draft guidance *Psychedelic Drugs: Considerations for Clinical Investigations* in June 2023; a final guidance was reported published in July 2026, with a companion *New England Journal of Medicine* commentary by four CDER officials on 10 September 2026 laying out a "new framework" — including receptiveness to active comparators (e.g., a low dose of the same psychedelic) to address functional unblinding, and to initial approval on ~12 weeks of efficacy evidence with longer blinded follow-up for retreatment safety. The final-guidance publication and NEJM commentary are **reported via secondary legal press; verify against fda.gov / NEJM before citing externally.**
Source: https://www.lexblog.com/2026/10/26/tell-me-what-you-want-what-you-really-really-want-fdas-top-officials-put-their-psychedelics-position-in-writing/ (as of 2026-11-07)

### 2.4 Oregon — regulated service model (not legalization, not medicine)

**Measure 109 (Oregon Psilocybin Services Act)**, approved by voters November 2020, codified as ORS 475A, directs the Oregon Health Authority (OHA) to license and regulate manufacture, transport, delivery, sale, purchase, and provision of psilocybin services. License applications opened 2 January 2023; the first license issued March 2023; service centers began opening to clients in summer 2023. Four license types: manufacturer, facilitator, service center, testing laboratory (plus worker permits). As of 1 February 2026 (Oregon Employment Department / OHA data): **11 manufacturers, 1 testing laboratory, 23 service centers, 389 licensed facilitators**, 935 worker permits issued.

What the model actually is:
- Adults **21+** may participate **without a prescription, diagnosis, or Oregon residency**.
- A licensed facilitator is present through **preparation → administration → integration** sessions.
- Psilocybin may **only** be consumed at a licensed service center under a facilitator — **no take-home, no retail sale**; personal possession outside a licensed center remains illegal.
- Facilitators complete a state-approved training program (160+ hours, OHA-approved curriculum); the role is supportive and non-directive, **not** medical treatment and **not** psychotherapy.
- The state does not set prices; reported session-cycle costs run roughly **$1,000–$3,500** (reported range, not an official figure; insurance does not cover it).

Oregon's model is a **state-regulated service framework**, not federal legalization and not a medical program. Psilocybin remains Schedule I federally.

Sources: https://digitalcollections.library.oregon.gov/assets/displaypdf/315487 (Oregon Employment Dept / OHA, Feb 2026); https://scienceinsights.org/where-is-psilocybin-therapy-legal-states-and-countries/ (as of 2026-11-07)

### 2.5 Colorado — decriminalization plus regulated healing centers

**Proposition 122 (Natural Medicine Health Act)**, approved November 2022, did two distinct things: (1) **decriminalized** personal use, possession, growth, and sharing of five natural medicines (psilocybin, psilocyn, ibogaine, mescaline excluding peyote, DMT) for adults 21+; and (2) created a **regulated program** — initially for psilocybin and psilocyn only — of licensed healing centers with supervised administration. SB23-290 assigned licensing to the Department of Revenue; applications opened 31 December 2024; the **first healing-center license was issued 31 March 2025** (reported). Unlike Oregon, Colorado permits personal use and cultivation alongside the licensed-center model; regulated centers are for supervised use, not retail sale.

Source: https://www.trueeira.com/legal-landscape (as of 2026-11-07); https://healingmaps.com/comparing-psilocybin-access-programs-in-oregon-colorado-and-new-mexico/ (as of 2026-11-07)

### 2.6 Elsewhere (for completeness, reported only)

- **Australia** is the only country reported to allow prescription of MDMA for PTSD (and psilocybin for treatment-resistant depression) through a legal clinical route — authorized prescribers under the Therapeutic Goods Administration's Schedule 8 rescheduling. **Verify the TGA dates and prescriber counts against TGA sources before citing externally.**
- **Canada:** MDMA and psilocybin access is via Health Canada's Special Access Program (SAP) only, case by case.
- Several U.S. cities (Denver, Oakland, Santa Cruz, Ann Arbor, Seattle, Washington D.C.) have decriminalized personal possession at the municipal level; decriminalization is not a regulated service model and not legalization.

## 3. The therapy model — screening → preparation → administration → integration

This section describes the model **as structured in the CPT/HCPCS coding guide for psychedelic-assisted therapy** (the guide the program holds on file), not as a clinical protocol the lab endorses. The guide organizes care into four stages, each with its own coding logic. The guide's code descriptions and RVUs are **2023 Medicare non-facility** values; the administration-session codes are Category III tracking codes (see Section 5).

Source document (read directly, 7 pages): `~/workspace/user/files/A-Guide-to-CPT-and-HCPCS-Codes-for-Psychedelic-Assisted-Therapy_Charts.pdf`

### Stage 1 — Screening, assessment, and intake

Purpose: determine eligibility, rule out contraindications, establish the clinical baseline. In the trials cited in Section 1, typical exclusions included history of psychotic disorder, serious suicide attempt, or hospitalization (Davis 2020) — screening is where those exclusions are applied.

Guide codes:
- **Psychiatric diagnostic evaluation:** 90791 (without medical services) / 90792 (with medical services); add-on +90785 for interactive complexity.
- **Psychological/neuropsychological testing:** 96130 (+96131 per additional hour) evaluation by physician/QHP; 96136 (+96137) test administration/scoring by physician/QHP; 96138 (+96139) by technician; brief assessments 96127 (per standardized instrument) and 96146 (single automated instrument via electronic platform).
- **E/M (E/M-eligible clinicians only):** 99202–99205 (new patient) / 99212–99215 (established patient).

### Stage 2 — Preparation

Purpose: build therapeutic alliance, set expectations and intentions, teach the participant what to expect during the administration session, and plan safety/support. In the COMPASS and MAPS protocols this is multiple sessions before dosing; in the Oregon service model it is a preparation session with the licensed facilitator.

Guide codes (choice depends on clinician type, session duration, and payer negotiation):
- **Psychotherapy:** 90832 (30 min), 90834 (45 min), 90837 (60 min); add-on +90785 for interactive complexity.
- **With E/M:** 99212–99215 + psychotherapy add-ons +90833 / +90836 / +90838.
- **Group:** 90853 group psychotherapy, with modifier 59 (distinct procedural service) where applicable.
- **Two licensed psychotherapists:** the guide suggests negotiating use of modifier XP (distinct service performed by a different practitioner), and negotiating to bill 90834 twice for a 90-minute session. **These are payer-negotiation suggestions in the guide, not payment guarantees.**

### Stage 3 — Administration session

Purpose: the supervised dosing session, with continuous in-person monitoring and intervention (psychotherapy, crisis intervention) as needed, for the full duration of the drug's effects (hours-long for psilocybin/LSD; the Oregon model describes 6–8 hour sessions).

Guide codes:
- **Category III tracking codes (per hour):** **0820T** — continuous in-person monitoring and intervention during psychedelic medication therapy, first physician or other qualified health care professional, each hour; **+0821T** — second physician/QHP, concurrent; **+0822T** — clinical staff under the direction of a physician/QHP, concurrent.
- **Medical oversight only (E/M-eligible clinicians):** 99212–99215 + prolonged-service add-ons +99415 / +99416 / +99417 where time thresholds are met.
- **Team-based compensation:** **H2020** (comprehensive medication services, per 15 minutes; not payable by Medicare) — the guide suggests negotiating a team-based rate to compensate all providers, including medical oversight billed separately.

### Stage 4 — Integration

Purpose: post-session psychotherapy to process the experience, consolidate any change, and monitor safety over follow-up. The guide states integration uses the **same code options as preparation** (90832/90834/90837, E/M + add-ons, group 90853). In the trials, integration is where durability is (or is not) established — the FDA's CRL specifically faulted the MDMA program for not demonstrating effect durability beyond 18 weeks, which is an integration/follow-up design question as much as a drug question.

### What the model is not

- The Oregon facilitator model is **not** this therapy model: Oregon facilitators provide non-directive support, not psychotherapy, and Oregon services are not medical treatment.
- The MM120 Phase 3 program tested the drug **without** psychotherapy — the field has not settled whether the therapy component is essential, and the answer may differ by compound.
- Coding a session is not the same as getting paid for it (Section 5).

## 4. Safety

### 4.1 What the trials show — and what they cannot show

- **Common adverse events** in the psilocybin Phase 2b (Goodwin 2022): headache, nausea, dizziness; 77% of participants had at least one adverse event. In the MM120 Phase 2b, visual perceptual changes were near-universal at active doses (92.5% at 100 µg), with nausea 40% and headache 35%.
- **Suicidality:** suicidal ideation, behavior, or intentional self-injury occurred across **all** dose groups including the 1 mg control in Goodwin 2022. In COMP005, the DSMB found no clinically meaningful imbalance in suicidality between arms. Suicidal participants are a high-risk population in these trials; safety conclusions depend on the screening exclusions (Section 3, Stage 1) and on monitoring that the trials provided.
- **Blinding / expectancy:** participants and often therapists can tell who received the active dose of a psychoactive drug. This functional unblinding inflates apparent effect sizes and was a central FDA concern in the MDMA CRL. Any honest reading of effect sizes in Section 1 must carry this caveat.
- **Long-term safety data are thin:** the longest controlled follow-up in the cited trials is weeks to months; durability beyond 18 weeks was specifically not demonstrated for MDMA; post-hoc and open-label durability claims (COMP005/COMP006 Part B/C) are provisional.
- **Contraindication logic in trials:** psychosis history, serious suicide attempt, and unstable medical conditions were exclusions. The trials therefore say nothing about safety in those populations.

### 4.2 The lab's screening layer — Snodgrass liver-stem-cell toxicity typing

**The method, from the patent itself.** U.S. Patent **8,512,957 B2**, *"Toxicity Typing Using Liver Stem Cells,"* inventor **H. Ralph Snodgrass** (San Jose, CA), assignee **Vistagen Therapeutics, Inc.** (South San Francisco, CA), granted **20 August 2013**, 25 claims (Application No. 13/401,623, filed 21 February 2012; divisional of Application No. 11/445,733, filed 1 June 2006, which was a continuation of Application No. 09/881,526, filed 14 June 2001). Title page and abstract verified by direct rendering of the patent PDF, 2026-11-07.

The abstract states the invention *"provides methods and systems for identifying and typing toxicity of chemical compositions, as well as for screening new compositions for toxicity. The invention involves detecting alterations in gene or protein expression and hence establishing molecular profiles in isolated mammalian liver stem cells [LSCs] contacted with various chemical compositions of known and unknown toxicities, and correlating the molecular profiles with toxicities of the chemical compositions."*

In plain terms, the method is:
1. **Isolate mammalian liver stem cells (LSCs)** — a proliferative, undifferentiated liver cell population.
2. **Expose** the LSCs to a test compound, alongside reference compounds of **known** toxicity.
3. **Measure alterations in gene and/or protein expression** in the exposed cells, establishing a **molecular profile** (expression signature) for each compound.
4. **Correlate** the test compound's profile against the reference profiles to **identify and type** its toxicity — i.e., not just "toxic / not toxic," but what *kind* of toxicity the signature resembles — and thereby **screen new compositions** before any animal or human exposure.

**Role in this program (proposed, not established):** the Snodgrass LSC method is the lab's **preclinical screening layer** for the whole compound library, including any psychedelic candidate. It sits *before* any regulatory submission and *before* any human work: a candidate whose LSC expression profile matches a known hepatotoxic signature is deprioritized or redesigned; a candidate with a clean profile advances to the next validation step. It is a **screening and typing tool**, not a safety proof: an in-vitro liver-stem-cell profile does not establish human safety, does not predict psychoactive or psychiatric risk, and does not substitute for the clinical safety data the FDA requires (the MDMA CRL turned in part on exactly that kind of clinical safety-data gap).

Its term runs from the 2001 parent filing (35 U.S.C. § 154), so it may already be expired — **verify current status via USPTO before relying on it** for exclusivity, licensing, or freedom-to-operate, and never assume in-force status from the grant date. ### 4.3 Safety governance the program must have before any human work

- DEA Schedule I researcher registration and FDA IND authorization — no human administration without both.
- Independent safety monitoring (a DSMB equivalent) and pre-registered adverse-event definitions that capture "positive/favorable" events as the FDA required in the CRL — the MDMA program's failure to do so was a cited deficiency.
- Pre-specified suicidality monitoring and stopping rules; exclusion criteria at least as strict as the cited trials until evidence supports widening them.
- Long-term follow-up designed in from the start (the CRL's durability objection must be answered prospectively, not retrospectively).

## 6. Research agenda — what the lab would actually do

### 6.1 Proposed program (none of this exists yet)

1. **Evidence synthesis (months 0–3).** Systematic review and evidence grading of the trials in Section 1 plus the wider literature (including negative and null results — e.g., Carhart-Harris 2021's non-significant primary outcome). Deliverable: a living evidence table with effect sizes, sample sizes, follow-up durations, and blinding limitations. *Validation: independent second-reviewer check of every extracted number against its source.*
2. **Preclinical toxicity screening (months 0–12, ongoing).** Apply the Snodgrass LSC method (Section 4.2) to the compound library, starting with non-psychedelic candidates and any psychedelic-adjacent compounds the program holds. Build reference molecular profiles from compounds of known toxicity before typing unknowns. *Validation: blinded re-testing of known-toxicity reference compounds; concordance rate reported before any candidate is typed.*
3. **Therapy-model design study (months 3–9).** A design-only workstream (no human dosing) that specifies, for each candidate indication, the screening criteria, preparation protocol, administration-session staffing and monitoring plan, and integration/follow-up schedule — structured on the CPT guide's four stages (Section 3) and stress-tested against the CRL's three deficiency areas (safety-data capture, durability follow-up, blinding). *Validation: external review by a clinician and a regulatory consultant; the design must name its own falsification criteria.*
4. **Regulatory-path preparation (months 6–18).** Pre-IND meeting preparation for the strongest candidate only, if and when the preclinical and evidence work supports it; DEA Schedule I researcher registration groundwork; state-model observation (Oregon/Colorado licensed services as observational settings only — the lab does not administer, facilitate, or refer into them). *Validation: written FDA feedback, not internal optimism, gates any IND filing.*
5. **Reimbursement and economics (parallel).** Work from the companion annex: payer interviews, cost-modeling of the four-stage model using the guide's codes, and a coverage-evidence plan. *Validation: at least one payer's written statement of what evidence it would require; no revenue forecast is published without it.*
6. **Safety surveillance design (parallel).** Adverse-event taxonomy that captures "positive/favorable" events (the CRL lesson), suicidality monitoring, and a long-term follow-up registry design with pre-specified durability endpoints beyond 18 weeks.

### 6.2 What NOT to claim

- **Never** claim any psychedelic treats, cures, or diagnoses depression, PTSD, anxiety, or any other condition. The trials report symptom-score changes in research settings; that is not a treatment claim.
- **Never** present Breakthrough Therapy designation as approval, or a CRL as proof a drug "doesn't work." Both misreadings are in circulation; both are wrong.
- **Never** present press-release topline results (COMP005, COMP006, MM120 Phase 3) as peer-reviewed findings, and never quote a single-arm response/remission percentage without the control arm next to it.
- **Never** present the Oregon or Colorado models as federal legalization, as medical treatment, or as an endorsement of efficacy.
- **Never** present the Snodgrass LSC screen as proof of human safety, and never present the patent as in-force or as the lab's property without a verified status check and, if needed, a license.
- **Never** present a reimbursement code as a payment guarantee, and never forecast revenue from Category III tracking codes.
- **Never** offer, facilitate, refer into, or imply access to psychedelic treatment. This program is research, therapy-model design, and reimbursement landscape — nothing more, until and unless a regulator authorizes a specific next step in writing.
- **Never** invent studies, statistics, people, or capabilities to fill a gap. Where this brief could not verify something, Section 7 says so.
