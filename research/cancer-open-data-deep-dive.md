# The Open Cancer Data Landscape: A Research-Resources Guide

*AGI Future Foundation · Bio Research Expansion · November 2026*

## What is true, and what is not yet true

Cancer is not one disease. It is hundreds of diseases that share a mechanism — cells growing and dividing without the normal controls — and differ in almost everything else: where they start, which genes drive them, how they respond to treatment, and how they are studied. That is why cancer research runs on data at a scale no single laboratory could produce — and why the open cancer data landscape matters: the largest cancer datasets are public resources, built with public money, available to any researcher who follows their access terms.

This article is a guide to those resources — what exists, what is open, what is controlled, and how to use each one honestly. It is a research-resources guide, not medical advice. It makes no claim about any treatment, cure, or diagnosis, and it does not recommend any course of action for any person. Approvals mentioned here are regulatory facts about specific drugs for specific indications; they are not endorsements, and they are not evidence about any other compound.

What is not yet true must be stated first, because it frames everything else. **No cancer product exists in this programme.** There is no cancer dataset, no cancer research, and no cancer tool on this machine. A full inventory of the machine in November 2026 found zero cancer research content anywhere: "cancer" appeared only as the Cancer DAO world name (anchored at the Fred Hutchinson Cancer Center in Seattle), one line in a compound-library article (natural-product cancer drugs as an example class), one line in a screening article (cervical cancer screening as the classic example of screening done as a programme), and a funder name in contacts files (the Cancer Prevention and Research Institute of Texas). One ALS paper with oncology relevance arrived later the same day (see §9). The "Cancer DAO" is only a world name with a Fred Hutchinson anchor and a promo short — not a product, not a dataset, not a research programme, and not a series with a number. This article is therefore entirely new content, built from verified open resources, and it does not describe anything this programme has built. Its honest purpose is narrower: to map the public resources that exist, so that any future cancer work in this programme starts from what is real.

## 1. Why cancer data is open — and why some of it is not

Cancer research has a structural problem that open data solves. A single hospital sees too few patients with any one cancer type, carrying any one mutation, to learn anything reliable. The answers live in aggregation: thousands of patients, across many institutions, with their tumours sequenced, their treatments recorded, and their outcomes followed. No single institution can assemble that alone, so the field built shared infrastructure — and because much of that infrastructure was built with public money, through the US National Cancer Institute, it is public by design.

But "public" does not mean "downloadable by anyone, no questions asked". Cancer data sits on a spectrum, and the spectrum is the subject of this article. At one end are fully open resources: processed genomic data, visualisation portals, trial registries, and aggregate statistics anyone can browse and download. At the other end are controlled-access resources: individual-level sequence data, germline variants, and clinical free text, which can identify a person and must be handled under governance. In between are the most common cases: open exploration with controlled download, free access with registration, and aggregate data that is public while the underlying individual records are not.

The distinction is not bureaucracy. It is the same distinction that runs through the whole open-data landscape in this programme: genomic data is identifying in a way that most data is not, and the participants who gave their data consented to research use under governance, not to their genomes being downloadable by anyone, for any purpose, forever. The openSNP story is the cautionary tale — a fully open, public-domain genomics site that shut down in April 2025 and deleted all user data, its founder judging that the risk-benefit calculus of free and open access to individual genetic data had changed fundamentally since 2011. The cancer field's answer is the same as the ALS field's: share deeply, but share through portals, request processes, and governance — not through open download of individual-level data.

## 2. NCI, TCGA, and the Genomic Data Commons — the anchor

The National Cancer Institute (NCI) is the US federal government's principal agency for cancer research, part of the National Institutes of Health. It runs the resources that anchor this article: the Genomic Data Commons (GDC), the Childhood Cancer Data Initiative (CCDI), and the Surveillance, Epidemiology, and End Results (SEER) programme. If there is one institution whose resources a cancer researcher should know first, it is NCI — not because it does everything, but because it holds the largest public cancer datasets and sets the access terms for most of them.

**TCGA — The Cancer Genome Atlas.** TCGA is the large-scale collaborative effort organised by NCI and the National Human Genome Research Institute, launched in 2006 and completed in 2018, to characterise cancer at the molecular level using genomics. Its legacy continues through follow-up initiatives, notably the Pan-Cancer Atlas and the NCI Genomic Data Commons. The scale, from NCI's bioinformatics training material (accessed November 2026): about 2.5 petabytes of genomic, epigenomic, transcriptomic, and proteomic data, plus clinical information, from over 11,000 patients representing 33 cancer types. That scale is the point: TCGA is the dataset that made it possible to study cancer as a molecular disease across types, rather than one tumour at a time.

Access to TCGA data is where the open-versus-controlled distinction first bites. **Not all TCGA data is open access.** Open-access data can be downloaded freely; data unique to an individual — individual germline variant data, primary sequence (.bam) files, clinical free text, exon array files — are controlled access, requiring certification through NCBI's dbGaP Authorized Access. The primary hub is the NCI Genomic Data Commons; legacy (non-GRCh38) datasets are no longer archived by the GDC — use the Broad GDAC Firehose / FireBrowse or the cBioPortal DataHub for those. The original TCGA Data Portal is listed as closed in 2026 (re3data, last update 8 September 2026), succeeded by the GDC. A researcher who goes looking for "the TCGA portal" and finds it closed has not lost the data; the data moved, and the GDC is where it lives now.

**The NCI Genomic Data Commons (GDC).** The GDC is NCI's repository and computational resource for cancer genomic data and associated clinical information. It includes data from 22 programmes (including TCGA), 83 projects, 69 primary sites, and 44,637 cases (NCI training page, November 2026). Data access and downloads are free, and no login is required except for controlled data (dbGaP authorisation, eRA Commons login). The portal is at https://portal.gdc.cancer.gov/.

That the data is still heavily used is visible in NCI's own figures: in the Childhood Cancer Data Initiative download table for January 2026, TCGA-BRCA (breast cancer) was the most-downloaded dataset at 755.95 terabytes, with CCDI-MCI second at 592.57 terabytes. A dataset completed in 2018, still the most-downloaded resource in 2026, shows what durable infrastructure looks like: the value of a shared dataset compounds, because every new question can be asked against data that already exists.

## 3. cBioPortal — exploration first, download second

If the GDC is where cancer genomic data lives, cBioPortal is where most researchers first meet it. cBioPortal is an open platform for exploratory, interactive visualisation, analysis, and download of cancer genomics data, built at Memorial Sloan Kettering with Dana-Farber and others, and funded in part by NCI. Users can view genomic alterations across patients and cancer types, run survival analysis and group comparisons, and explore by gene, mutation, or cancer type. It is best for exploration; for raw mRNA expression or full segmented copy-number files, use the GDC.

That division of labour is how the field's infrastructure is designed. The GDC holds the data; cBioPortal holds processed, curated datasets and the tools to look at them. A researcher asking "how often is this gene altered in this cancer, and does it track with survival?" starts in cBioPortal, not in a 2.5-petabyte download; a researcher who wants raw data for a new analysis goes to the GDC. Neither replaces the other, and confusing them is the most common beginner mistake in this landscape.

Access is open for exploration and download of the processed datasets cBioPortal hosts; it does not hold controlled raw sequence data. NCI also runs a CCDI instance at cbioportal.ccdi.cancer.gov, for childhood cancer data. The number of studies and datasets hosted on cBioPortal was not pinned to a single verified figure in the research for this article, so this article does not quote one — a count that changes with every release is a count that should be read on the portal, not in an article. The CCDI reports 40 multimodal paediatric and adolescent/young-adult studies currently available (March 2026 symposium), which is the verified figure for the childhood side.

The tooling around cBioPortal is open source, which matters for a programme like this one. The main platform repository is [cBioPortal/cbioportal](https://github.com/cBioPortal/cbioportal) (Apache 2.0, active November 2026, 1,064 stars), with a React frontend ([cBioPortal/cbioportal-frontend](https://github.com/cBioPortal/cbioportal-frontend), active November 2026), a curated public datasets repository ([cBioPortal/datahub](https://github.com/cBioPortal/datahub), active November 2025), and a Docker Compose setup for running a local instance ([cBioPortal/cbioportal-docker-compose](https://github.com/cBioPortal/cbioportal-docker-compose), active October 2026). For working with TCGA data in R, the standard toolkit is TCGAbiolinks ([BioinformaticsFMRP/TCGAbiolinks](https://github.com/BioinformaticsFMRP/TCGAbiolinks), active October 2026, 357 stars), a Bioconductor package for TCGA data access and analysis. Unlike ALS — where the GitHub research for this programme found no major dedicated research repository — cancer genomics has a mature open-source tooling layer, and it is one of the reasons the field moves as fast as it does.

## 4. SEER — the registry anchor

If TCGA and the GDC answer the question "what does the tumour look like at the molecular level?", SEER answers a different question: "how many people get this cancer, who are they, and what happens to them?" SEER — Surveillance, Epidemiology, and End Results — is NCI's population-based cancer registry programme, funded continuously since 1973. It covers 17 core registries plus 11 research-support registries across 22 geographic areas, covering about 45.9% of the US population (IntuitionLabs, August 2026, citing seer.cancer.gov).

SEER is the registry anchor for the same reason TCGA is the genomics anchor: it is the largest, longest-running, most standardised source of its kind. Fifty years of continuous funding means fifty years of comparable data — incidence, stage at diagnosis, treatment, and survival, collected the same way, year after year. That comparability is what makes trends visible: whether a cancer is becoming more common, whether it is being caught earlier, whether survival is improving, and for whom. A single year's data can mislead; fifty years of standardised data rarely does.

Access terms are unusually generous for a resource of this kind. Standard research files are free to any user with a valid email address. The linked SEER-Medicare product — SEER data linked to Medicare claims — is not a public-use file and carries direct cost-recovery fees. That split is the pattern again: the aggregate and de-identified research files are open; the linked, individual-level product is gated and fee-bearing. A researcher planning to use SEER should start with the standard research files, which are free, and only consider SEER-Medicare if the research question genuinely requires linked claims data — and budget for the fees.

## 5. ClinicalTrials.gov — the registry of record, and its limits

ClinicalTrials.gov is the registry and results database of privately and publicly funded clinical studies worldwide, run by the US National Library of Medicine (NIH). It is the place to find out what trials exist, what they are testing, who can enrol, and — for trials that report results — what happened. For a field where the distance between a laboratory finding and a patient is measured in trials, the registry is the map of that distance.

Its scale is large, and counts differ by date and definition, so they must be quoted carefully. A 2026 EACL paper's table gives 543,172 "all studies", with 60,449 completed with results; Wikipedia gives over 444,000 trials from 221 countries; a 2025 landscape paper gives more than 345,000 studies. This article uses "over 500,000 registrations (2026)" with the EACL source and does not mix the figures into one number: the counts were taken at different dates, with different definitions, and a registry growing by thousands of records a month will always produce different counts on different days.

The registry is free to search, and its aggregate database AACT is publicly available for aggregate analysis. Registration is required for many trials by law and policy, but many trials remain unregistered, so the registry is not a census of all clinical research. That is an important limit: absence from ClinicalTrials.gov does not prove a trial does not exist, and presence does not prove a trial is well run. The registry is a record of what was registered, not a quality judgement.

Cancer-specific use shows both its power and its blind spots. A Frontiers in Epidemiology landscape analysis (October 2026) examined cancer prevention and interception studies registered through September 2025: 296 retrieved, 209 eligible, 75.1% interventional — and found the field "structurally invisible" in registry categories, with 87.6% of records under generic "Other" funding. Prevention and interception — stopping cancer before it starts, or catching it at its earliest stage — is one of the most important directions in cancer research, and the registry's categories do not yet describe it well; a researcher who searches only the obvious categories will miss most of it.

## 6. FDA approvals — the regulatory landscape, dated and specific

The US Food and Drug Administration maintains a live Cancer Accelerated Approvals listing — ongoing approvals with postmarketing requirements to verify clinical benefit — and per-drug approval notices. The approvals below are regulatory facts about specific drugs for specific indications. They are not endorsements, they are not evidence about any other compound, and they are not a claim that any cancer is cured or treatable in general. Each is dated, and each states its indication, because an approval without an indication is a misleading sentence.

Recent examples from the listing and notices, all 2026, all for the specific indication stated:

- **Gedatolisib (Revtorpyk, Celcuity)** plus fulvestrant, with or without palbociclib, for HR-positive, HER2-negative locally advanced or metastatic breast cancer without a PIK3CA mutation — approved 14 July 2026 (VIKTORIA-1, NCT05501886, 392 adults).
- **Dostarlimab (Jemperli)** for Stage II/III locally advanced dMMR/MSI-H rectal cancer — accelerated approval 19 October 2026 (AZUR-1, NCT05723562).
- **Sevabertinib (Hyrnuo)** for HER2 (ERBB2) TKD-mutant non-squamous non-small cell lung cancer — accelerated approval 9 September 2026 (SOHO-02).
- **Amivantamab plus hyaluronidase (Rybrevant Faspro)** for recurrent or metastatic head-and-neck squamous cell carcinoma — accelerated approval 5 November 2026 (OrigAMI-5).

A 2026 oncology round-up also lists early-2026 approvals and expansions including nivolumab plus chemotherapy for untreated Hodgkin lymphoma (20 March 2026), vepdegestrant for ESR1-mutated ER-positive, HER2-negative breast cancer (1 May 2026), and zongertinib for HER2-TKD non-small cell lung cancer (26 February 2026), among others (JADPRO FDA Focus, 2026).

Two honesty points belong here. First, "accelerated approval" is a specific regulatory status, not a synonym for "approved". Accelerated approval rests on a surrogate endpoint — a measure thought to predict clinical benefit — with postmarketing requirements to verify that benefit. An accelerated approval can be withdrawn if the confirmatory trial does not verify benefit; the ALS field's Relyvrio, approved in 2022 and withdrawn in 2024, is the cautionary example from a neighbouring disease. Second, no verified total count of FDA oncology approvals for 2025, or for 2026 year-to-date, was established in the research for this article. The FDA pages list approvals individually. This article does not aggregate them into a yearly total, because no verified total exists to aggregate. A count invented by adding up a partial list would be worse than no count at all.

## 8. What is open, what is controlled — and the model to copy

The pattern across every resource in this article is the same, and it is worth stating once, plainly. Processed, aggregate, and de-identified data is open: anyone can browse it, download it, and build on it. Individual-level data — germline variants, primary sequence files, clinical free text, linked claims — is controlled: available to qualified researchers under governance, through dbGaP, through a portal, through a request process. Exploration is open; raw download is governed. That is not a flaw in the system. It is the system, and it is the reason the system has survived fifty years of public trust.

The model to copy is GA4GH's "data visiting": shared data is analysed inside the provider's computing environment rather than downloaded — the direction of travel for UK Biobank, All of Us, and the cancer field's controlled-access resources. Any proposed data-sharing work in this programme — including the proposed Genomic DAO (S27) and Genome.Blockchain (S28), both proposed, not formed — should be framed as consent-gated, federated, and GA4GH-aligned, not as open download; the openSNP closure is the evidence for why.

The same discipline applies to compound data, which is the bridge between this article and the ALS deep-dive. ChEMBL (CC BY-SA 3.0, version 37, prepared 1 May 2026: 2,921,148 compounds, 24,527,044 activities) and PubChem (public domain, about 124 million compounds as of October 2026) are safe open foundations for a compound library, with source, version, and date attribution. DrugBank (CC BY-NC 4.0, academic downloads paused June 2026) is the trap: do not build a commercial compound product on it without a commercial licence. None of these databases is a treatment resource. They are chemistry and bioactivity data, and the "not a medical device" line must stay attached to any compound output — in cancer as in ALS.

## 9. The "Cancer DAO" and this programme's own position

This section exists because the name will confuse someone if it is not addressed directly. "Cancer DAO" appears in this programme in three places: as a world in the WorldForge and worlds-3d collections, anchored at the Fred Hutchinson Cancer Center in Seattle (47.6263, -122.3292); as a promo short in the bright-brand video sets; and as one of six PBC focus-domain DAOs named in an October 2026 note (Cancer, Robotics, Ecology, Climate, Education, Public Health). That is the whole of it. The Cancer DAO is a world name with a Fred Hutchinson anchor. It is not a product, not a dataset, not a research programme, not a DAO with members or governance, and not a series with a number. There is no Cancer DAO code, no Cancer DAO data, and no Cancer DAO funding on this machine, and this article does not invent any of it.

One file arrived after that inventory, and it should be recorded here rather than ignored. On 7 November 2026 the machine received "Synergistic Neuroprotection in ALS: Akt/Bad Phosphorylation as a Predictive Biomarker for BCL-2 Family Therapy" (als_4_8.pdf), a computational-model paper by x@agicorp.network and Bios. Its subject is ALS — AGI-183, a BCL-XL stabilizer, combined with AAV6-hSyn-MCL1 in iPSC-derived TDP-43 motor neurons — but its central mechanism is shared with oncology: the BCL-2 family, BH3 profiling, and Akt/Bad phosphorylation as a proposed predictive biomarker for BCL-2 family therapy in neurodegeneration. That narrow proposal had no peer-reviewed counterexample in a November 2026 verification search, but prior art exists at its boundary: a granted 2012 patent (WO2012160563) proposed Akt phosphorylation as a prognostic biomarker in neurodegenerative disease, and Léger et al. (2006) and Dewil et al. (2007) already reported reduced phospho-Akt in ALS. In oncology the biomarker evidence is preclinical only — cell-line work (Rice et al. 2019) marking phosphorylated AKT as a sensitivity marker, not a clinically validated biomarker. The paper also reviews the troubled history of systemic MCL1 inhibitors: most programmes were discontinued, cardiotoxicity the predominant dose-limiting factor, but not uniformly — PRT1419 remains in active development with acceptable safety notably without cardiac toxicity in solid tumours, and GS-9716's failure mode is undocumented. For AZD5991: in a post hoc retrospective high-sensitivity troponin T analysis, troponin was elevated in 54/65 (83%) patients after any dose versus 14/31 (45%) before any dose, while prospectively reported elevations were 8/78 (10.3%); and the 3/78 overall response (3.9%) was entirely in MDS patients (3/5, 60%), with no responses in AML (0/38) or other histologies (0/35). Its five discoveries are computational-model predictions, not wet-lab results, and the paper makes no treatment or cure claim. It is the first cancer-relevant research file on this machine — not a cancer product, a Cancer DAO, or a dataset — and it does not change the status stated above. But the ALS–oncology bridge it proposes is now the most concrete cancer connection this programme has, and any future cancer work should start by verifying its citations rather than repeating its predictions. No "ALS DAO" or "Cancer DAO" launch is stated in the paper itself; that framing is not adopted here.

The same honesty applies to the neighbouring bio series. The proposed W3 LLC sub-series in this programme's bio area are S4 Curable.X, S5 BioPharma.Go, S17 Curable.Blockchain DAO, S18 BioDAO.blockchain DAO, S27 Genomic DAO, and S28 Genome.Blockchain — all proposed, from an internal April 2026 list that is not a Wyoming SOS filing. None is formed, funded, or operating. The Foundation's site carries articles for several of them, each labelled "still undefined", and that label is accurate: the articles describe an intention, not a programme. The AGI-Biotech-tool repository — described as "biodao.blockchain", a molecular workspace for drug discovery in VR and browser, focused on neurogenetic targets including ALS and Parkinson's — is real code with real documentation, but it is a workspace, not a cancer product, and it does not contain cancer data.

What this programme does have, honestly, is a map. This article is the map for cancer: the public resources, their access terms, and their limits. Any future cancer work here should start from the GDC, cBioPortal, SEER, and ClinicalTrials.gov — citing them, using them under their terms, and building beside them rather than duplicating them. The field does not need another copy of TCGA. It needs tools that start from the data that already exists, cite it properly, and add organisation on top. That is the same lesson as the ALS deep-dive, and it is the only honest way to begin.

## 10. How to use these resources honestly

**Start with the question, not the dataset.** A dataset is not a finding. TCGA can tell you how often a gene is altered in a cancer; it cannot tell you whether a drug works. ClinicalTrials.gov can tell you what trials exist; it cannot tell you which one a person should join. SEER can tell you how common a cancer is and how survival has changed; it cannot tell you what will happen to any individual. The resource serves the question. Pick the question first.

**Read the access tier before you plan.** Open exploration (cBioPortal), open download of processed data (GDC open tier), free with registration (SEER standard files), controlled access (GDC controlled tier via dbGaP, SEER-Medicare with fees). A plan built on data you cannot access is not a plan. Check the tier first, and budget the time a request step takes — about two weeks in the ALS field's equivalent, and not instant here either.

**Cite the version and the date.** TCGA is a completed programme; the GDC is a living repository whose counts change with reprocessing. Quote each figure with its date and its source, and re-check it before any public use. A count without a date is a count that will be wrong soon.

**Do not aggregate what was not counted.** No verified total of FDA oncology approvals for 2025 or 2026 year-to-date was established in the research for this article, and none is quoted here. No single current count of cBioPortal studies was verified, and none is quoted. ClinicalTrials.gov counts differ by date and definition, and the spread is stated rather than resolved. An invented total is worse than an admitted gap.

**Keep approvals specific.** An approval is a drug, an indication, and a date. Gedatolisib is approved for a specific breast cancer population, not for "breast cancer" in general, and certainly not for cancer in general. Accelerated approval is a specific status with postmarketing requirements, not a synonym for approval. The moment an approval is quoted without its indication, it has become a misleading sentence.

**Use the real sources.** The resources in this article are public and free: the GDC portal, cBioPortal, SEER, ClinicalTrials.gov, and the FDA's approval pages. A claim that cannot be traced to one of those — or to a peer-reviewed paper — should be treated as unproven, however confidently it is stated.

## Frequently asked questions

**What is the largest open cancer dataset?** The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA), via the NCI Genomic Data Commons: about 2.5 petabytes from over 11,000 patients across 33 cancer types; the GDC as a whole holds 44,637 cases across 22 programmes (NCI training page, November 2026).

**Is all TCGA data open?** No. Open-access data can be downloaded freely, but individual-level data — germline variants, primary sequence files, clinical free text — is controlled access through NCBI dbGaP Authorized Access.

**What is cBioPortal for?** Exploration. It is an open platform for visualising and analysing processed cancer genomics data — alterations across patients and cancer types, survival analysis, group comparisons. For raw data, use the GDC.

**What is SEER?** NCI's population-based cancer registry programme, funded continuously since 1973, covering about 45.9% of the US population. Standard research files are free to any user with a valid email address; the linked SEER-Medicare product carries cost-recovery fees.

**Does this programme have a cancer product?** No. The "Cancer DAO" is only a world name with a Fred Hutchinson Cancer Center anchor. There is no cancer dataset, research, or tool on this machine. This article is a map of public resources, not a description of anything built here. It is not medical advice; anyone with a health question should speak with a clinician, not with an article.

## Glossary

- **AACT** — Aggregate Analysis of ClinicalTrials.gov, the registry's publicly available aggregate database.
- **Accelerated approval** — FDA approval based on a surrogate endpoint, with postmarketing requirements to verify clinical benefit.
- **CCDI** — NCI's Childhood Cancer Data Initiative; 40 multimodal paediatric and adolescent/young-adult studies available (March 2026).
- **cBioPortal** — open platform for exploration, visualisation, and analysis of processed cancer genomics data.
- **dbGaP** — NCBI's database of Genotypes and Phenotypes; Authorized Access governs controlled-access genomic data.
- **GDC** — NCI Genomic Data Commons, the repository for cancer genomic data and clinical information.
- **SEER** — Surveillance, Epidemiology, and End Results, NCI's population-based cancer registry programme since 1973.
- **TCGA** — The Cancer Genome Atlas, NCI/NHGRI programme (2006–2018), over 11,000 patients across 33 cancer types.
