## 2. The device as extracted (faithful to the patent — not our claims)

The patent covers a **hand-held remote control for a non-surgical spinal treatment (traction / spinal-decompression) table** — a positioning/control device, not a chiropractic adjustment instrument and not a Gonstead-specific device. The Gonstead connection is by program placement (Series 13), not by the patent's content: the patent never mentions Gonstead.

### 2a. The table (bed body 1)

- **Fixed bed body (20)** on a base (10) and pedestal (110), with two longitudinal slide rails (30).
- **Upper pulldown bed body (31)** at the patient's head end and **lower pulldown bed body (32)** at the foot end, both slidably mounted on the rails — they can move together or separately, staying in the same plane.
- **Bed-moving drive mechanism** — electric pushrod (310) between the fixed bed and the upper/lower beds — drives both movable beds together along the longitudinal (Y) direction relative to the fixed bed.
- **Bed-separation drive mechanism** — electric pushrod (350) with an end stopper passing through a slider (340) on the lower bed — drives the lower bed relative to the upper bed. Shortening pulls the lower bed close to the upper bed; extending frees the lower bed to slide, separating the two beds longitudinally. The patent's stated purpose: separating the beds removes most of the friction between the patient and the bed surface, increasing the traction dose-effect. (Patent assertion, not verified evidence.)
- **Upper-body positioning:** a pair of **armpit support arms (70)** with three adjustment mechanisms — vertical (Z, height), horizontal (X, across the body, i.e. the line between the patient's two armpits), and longitudinal (Y, along the body). The patent's stated purpose: position the upper body for patients of different heights/builds without relying on fixed straps (which it says cause strangulation sensation, poor circulation, dyspnoea, sweating and hygiene problems over 30+ minute sessions). (Patent assertion, not verified evidence.)
- **Lumbar airbag (401)** set into the surface of the upper mattress (41) at the patient's waist position, with trachea, air valve, and an inflation/deflation drive mechanism. Inflating supports the lumbar curve; deflating, the patent says, it does not fold or shift. (Patent assertion.)
- **Tilt mechanism:** an electric pushrod (150) in the base, pivoting the bed around a bearing (260). Extending raises the head end and lowers the foot end toward near-vertical; shortening returns it to flat (pushrod retracts into the base). Damping modules (rubber mounts or springs) at the supports. The patent's stated purpose: the patient mounts/dismounts while upright and is then lowered to flat, avoiding the muscle strain of sitting up after decompression treatment. (Patent assertion.)
- **Interfaces:** a data interface (D-type 9-pin in the embodiment) and a power interface; the remote connects by data cable.

### 2b. The hand-held remote (502)

- **Physical-button remote** (not a touchscreen, not an app), connected to the bed by a data cable; a wireless "Digiplex" variant is mentioned as a possible alternative. It docks in a **slot (501)** on the side of the bed via a protrusion on its back.
- **Internal modules** (circuit-board functional modules) and **external buttons**:
  - Bed control module — front adjustment button (601) and rear adjustment button (602) drive the separation mechanism (upper vs lower bed); open button (603) and close button (604) drive the moving mechanism (both beds together vs fixed bed).
  - Armpit support-arm control module — Z up (607) / Z down (608), X up (609) / X down (610), Y up (611) / Y down (612).
  - Inflation/deflation control module — inflate button (605) / vent button (606) for the lumbar airbag.
  - Electric-pushrod control module — extend button (613) / shorten button (614) for the tilt.
- **Stated benefit (abstract):** control signals pass to the bed control board and "automatically control all adjustment work before and after completed treatment, more time saving and energy saving." The patent also says it "can be applicable to other automatic traction or spinal column pressure reduction equipment."
- **Coordinate convention (patent's own):** X = horizontal (line between the patient's armpits); Y = longitudinal (along the patient); Z = vertical (perpendicular to the bed surface).

### 2c. What it covers, and its limits

- **Covers:** remote, wired control of a split-bed traction/decompression table — bed translation, bed separation, three-axis armpit-support positioning, lumbar-airbag inflation, and flat-to-upright tilt. Five claims: claim 1 (core remote + bed), claim 2 (docking slot), claim 3 (armpit support arms, X/Y/Z), claim 4 (lumbar airbag), claim 5 (tilt pushrod).
- **Does NOT cover:** any chiropractic adjustment technique, any Gonstead assessment or adjustment method, any diagnostic function, any sensor or feedback loop (the patent's background discusses patient feedback to a feedback system as a design goal, but the claims contain no sensor, no force measurement, no closed-loop control), any treatment protocol, any outcome, or any software. It is a 2014 wired-button remote for positioning a table. The background's traction/decompression physiology and condition list are unexamined assertions in a utility-model specification, not evidence.
- **Utility-model caveat:** Chinese utility models are granted without substantive examination; grant is not evidence of novelty, inventive step, or clinical validity.

## 4. Inspiration layer — remote-controlled, pre-programmed table positioning

*Design directions inspired by CN 204233258 U. These are hypotheses and designs, never the patent's claims and never our results. Each is labelled "inspired by [patent]" and carries a confirm-or-kill test and an evidence grade.*

**Evidence grades used:** A = peer-reviewed evidence; B = credible engineering precedent, no clinical evidence; C = hypothesis, untested.

### Direction 1 — Sensor-guided, pre-programmed positioning presets
**Inspired by CN 204233258 U** (hand-held remote driving all pre-/post-treatment table adjustments from one handset).

- **The idea:** a modern Gonstead treatment table whose positioning is pre-programmed per documented adjustment setup: the practitioner selects a named setup (e.g. cervical chair, pelvic bench, knee-chest) and the table moves — split-bed translation, separation, tilt, upper-body supports, lumbar support — to recorded positions, with position sensors confirming each axis reached its target before the practitioner proceeds. The 2014 design is open-loop buttons; the modern version closes the loop.
- **What a modern version would look like:** absolute position encoders on each axis; stored, named presets with per-patient anthropometric offsets (entered by the licensed practitioner, not inferred); a single handset or foot control; hard travel limits and a dead-man interlock; an audit log of every move (position, time, operator) for the patient record.
- **Confirm-or-kill test:** build a single-axis bench prototype (one actuated axis + encoder + preset recall); measure positioning repeatability (±mm) across 50 cycles and time-to-position vs manual setup. **Kill if** repeatability is worse than manual positioning or setup time is not reduced. **Confirm if** repeatability is within ±2 mm and setup time falls by ≥30% with zero safety interlock failures.
- **Evidence grade: C** (hypothesis; no prototype, no clinical data). The underlying mechanism — pre-set positioning — is standard industrial practice (grade B as engineering), but its value in a chiropractic setting is untested.

### Direction 2 — Documented adjustment-protocol table states
**Inspired by CN 204233258 U** (the patent's one-handset control of "all adjustment work before and after completed treatment").

- **The idea:** treat each table position as a *documented protocol state* rather than an ad-hoc setting: every named Gonstead setup gets a written specification (bed separation, tilt angle, support-arm X/Y/Z, lumbar support state) that is versioned, reviewed by licensed practitioners, and linked to the technique documentation in §3b(2). The table becomes a physical record of the protocol, not just furniture.
- **What a modern version would look like:** a protocol library (versioned documents, each with author, reviewer, date, and the literature it rests on); the table controller loads a protocol by name and reports its state against the specification; deviations are flagged, not silently accepted; protocols are publishable for independent review.
- **Confirm-or-kill test:** draft three protocol specifications with two licensed Gonstead practitioners and have a third practitioner, blinded, reproduce the setups from the documents alone on a manual table; measure inter-practitioner agreement on the resulting positions. **Kill if** agreement is poor (setups not reproducible from the documents) or practitioners judge the specifications clinically meaningless. **Confirm if** the blinded reproduction matches within agreed tolerances and practitioners rate the specifications usable.
- **Evidence grade: C** (hypothesis; no protocols drafted, no practitioners engaged). Depends entirely on licensed-practitioner validation that has not happened.

### Direction 3 — Patient-mount/dismount safety by design (upright-to-flat sequencing)
**Inspired by CN 204233258 U** (the tilt pushrod that lets the patient mount upright and be lowered to flat, and be raised again to leave — intended to avoid post-treatment sit-up strain).

- **The idea:** make the mount/dismount sequence a designed safety feature of the table, not an afterthought: a scripted, speed-limited upright→flat→upright cycle with the upper-body supports engaged throughout, a patient-held stop control, and a documented fall/transfer risk assessment. The 2014 patent's tilt is manually button-driven; the modern version makes the sequence itself the safety case.
- **What a modern version would look like:** a sequenced state machine (upright mount → supports set → lower to flat → treatment → raise → dismount) with a patient-accessible stop, speed limits on tilt, and a written risk assessment (hazards, mitigations, residual risk) reviewed by a licensed practitioner and a device-safety engineer.
- **Confirm-or-kill test:** run the sequence with 20 healthy volunteers of varied height/build on a prototype or an existing tilt table; record mount/dismount time, any loss-of-balance events, and subjective ease vs a fixed flat table. **Kill if** any balance event occurs or ease is not better than a flat table. **Confirm if** zero balance events and a clear ease advantage, with the risk assessment accepted by the reviewing practitioner and engineer.
- **Evidence grade: C** (hypothesis; no prototype, no volunteers, no risk assessment). The underlying hazard (post-treatment dizziness/strain on standing) is plausible but the benefit of sequencing is untested.
