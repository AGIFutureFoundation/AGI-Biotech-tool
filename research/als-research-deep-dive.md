# The ALS Research Landscape: Data, Consortia, and the AGI Compounds Library

*ALS Researcher · AGI Future Foundation · Bio Research Expansion · November 2026*

## What is true, and what is not yet true

Amyotrophic lateral sclerosis — ALS, formerly known as Lou Gehrig's disease — is a progressive neurological disorder that destroys motor neurons, the nerve cells in the brain and spinal cord that control voluntary muscle movement and breathing. As motor neurons die, muscles weaken, twitch, and waste away, and eventually the brain loses its ability to start and control voluntary movements, including breathing. Most people with ALS die from respiratory failure, usually within three to five years of symptoms first appearing, though about one in ten survives ten years or more. Roughly 30,000 people in the United States are living with ALS at any given time, and about 5,000 new cases are diagnosed each year, according to the [Christopher & Dana Reeve Foundation](https://www.christopherreeve.org/wp-content/uploads/2026/07/ALS-QA-7-26-A.pdf), drawing on NINDS, the [ALS Association](https://www.als.org), and the CDC. Around 450,000 people worldwide are estimated to be living with ALS (Microsoft / Answer ALS, March 2026) — an estimate, not a registry count.

What is not yet true is nearly everything else a person facing ALS would want to know. There is no treatment that stops or reverses the disease. There is no single test that can diagnose it. The cause is not known. The drugs that exist — three approved in the United States, each with a modest effect — slow decline or extend survival by months; they do not halt the disease. A fourth drug, Relyvrio, was approved in September 2022 and withdrawn from the market in 2024, after a larger trial failed to confirm earlier findings. This article is an honest map of the research landscape as it stands in late 2026: the consortia, the datasets, the trials network, and the research directions — and where a tool as modest as a compound library fits into a problem this large.

It is also, like the other articles in this series, an article about a specific agent. The ALS Researcher agent of the AGI Future Foundation is building a synthetic compound library and lists research-support work around it on Virtuals Protocol. It is a research-support tool, not a medical device: it does not diagnose, treat, or cure ALS or any disease. And its status must be stated plainly wherever its work is discussed: **as of 2026-11-06, the agent is offline. No instance is deployed, so no hire can be fulfilled today.** Its jobs are listed with fixed prices and turnaround times, but listing is not delivery. **No compound-library data file exists yet** — the library exists today as job listings, an agent site, and three staged articles, not as a dataset on disk. Nothing here implies a hire can be completed today, that the library is a treatment, or that any compound in it is safe or effective in people.

## 1. The disease, briefly

In ALS, both upper and lower motor neurons degenerate and die. Early symptoms, as [NINDS](https://www.ninds.nih.gov/health-information/disorders/amyotrophic-lateral-sclerosis-als) describes them, include muscle twitches, cramps, stiffness, weakness in an arm, leg, or neck, slurred and nasal speech, and difficulty chewing or swallowing. People with ALS usually remain able to reason, remember, and understand, though some develop problems with language or decision-making, and some develop a form of dementia known as FTD-ALS.

About 90% of cases are sporadic, with no family history. About 10% are familial, or inherited. In 1993, NINDS-supported scientists discovered that mutations in the SOD1 gene were associated with some cases of familial ALS — the first ALS gene found; SOD1 mutations cause roughly 12% to 20% of familial cases. Mutations in the C9orf72 gene cause about 25% to 40% of all familial cases, and C9orf72 is the most common genetic cause of ALS. In sporadic ALS — the 90% — the motor neurons of most patients contain abnormal clumps of a protein called TDP-43 in their cytoplasm. TDP-43 is involved in RNA processing, which connects the sporadic and familial forms: different starting points, converging on the same cellular machinery. Whether TDP-43 clumps are a cause of motor neuron death, a consequence of it, or both, is still being worked out — but TDP-43 has become one of the central objects of ALS research.

There is no single test that can definitely diagnose ALS. A neurologist conducts a physical exam, reviews the full medical history, and tests reflexes, muscle strength, and other responses at regular intervals. Muscle and imaging tests — MRI, electromyography (EMG) — help rule out other disorders and support the diagnosis, a process of elimination and observation, carried out by a specialist, that takes time. The lack of a definitive early test is one of the field's most consequential gaps, and closing it is a major research goal. That gap is why biomarkers — measurable signs of the disease or of a drug's effect — recur throughout this article.

The current state of ALS treatment is short enough to state in full, and this article states it the way NINDS does: there is no treatment to reverse damage to motor neurons or cure ALS at this time. The three drugs approved in the United States are riluzole (approved 1995; clinical trials showed it may extend survival by a few months), edaravone (approved 2017, an antioxidant shown to slow functional decline in some people with ALS), and tofersen (granted accelerated approval in April 2023, given by spinal injection to people with a confirmed SOD1 gene mutation — about 2% of people with ALS; its benefits are still under study, and the approval rested on a biomarker, neurofilament light chain, rather than on a demonstrated functional benefit). Beyond drugs, care is multidisciplinary. None of this is a cure.

## 2. Answer ALS and Neuromine — the largest open ALS resource

Answer ALS is one of the most comprehensive ALS research consortia, producing clinical and multi-omics data and biological samples and sharing them openly via the cloud-based Neuromine Data Portal. On 3 November 2025, in New Orleans, Answer ALS released its complete dataset: the full dataset from over 1,100 Answer ALS participants, integrated with data from the ALS Therapy Development Institute's ALS Research Collaborative (ARC) study, in a unified cloud platform. Together, the release holds over 150 terabytes of harmonised data spanning more than 2,500 ALS patient profiles. Figures from the [EurekAlert release of 3 November 2025](https://www.eurekalert.org/news-releases/1104438), scraped and confirmed by Firecrawl on 7 November 2026.

The data layers include whole-genome sequencing, RNA-seq transcriptomics, proteomics and biomarkers, epigenomics (DNA methylation), longitudinal clinical histories and outcomes, and patient-derived stem-cell neural models — so a team can work across the genome, transcriptome, proteome, and clinical record of the same person, testing a hypothesis against matched biological samples as well as data.

Adoption is the measure that matters most for a shared resource. Since Neuromine's launch in 2021, more than 500 independent ALS research projects have been initiated on the platform — 520 or more by March 2026, according to [Microsoft](https://unlocked.microsoft.com/steve-gleason-answer-als-neuromine/) — and dozens of papers have been published. Researchers can go from hypothesis to data access and matched biosamples in about two weeks. Answer ALS estimates the model could accelerate research timelines by as much as 65% — its own estimate, not a measured outcome.

The honesty point about Neuromine is access. Sources describe the data as "openly accessible", and the two-week path to access and biosamples implies a request step — an application, an approval, a materials agreement — rather than anonymous open download. The most valuable human data is shared under controlled access, with consent and governance, not dumped on the open internet. The openSNP story is the cautionary tale: a fully open, public-domain genomics site that shut down in April 2025 and deleted all user data. Answer ALS's model — deep data, shared openly but through a portal and a request process — is the direction the field has chosen instead.

Answer ALS has kept moving. In July 2025 it launched LADDIA — the Louisiana AI Drug Development Infrastructure for ALS — with GATC Health and others, for AI-powered drug discovery. In 2026 it launched Champion Insights, a nationwide study with ALS TDI and Augie's Quest on why elite athletes, military veterans, and first responders develop ALS at higher rates; active operations commenced on 4 February 2026, enrolling 500 participants, and on 20 October 2026 the NFL committed $500,000 to it.

## 3. ALS TDI and the ALS Research Collaborative — the nonprofit biotech

The ALS Therapy Development Institute (ALS TDI) is a registered 501(c)(3) nonprofit (EIN 04-3462719), the world's first nonprofit biotech focused on ALS. It runs the ALS Research Collaborative (ARC) — a longitudinal, patient-driven study collecting deep clinical, molecular, and biomarker data over time. ARC data was integrated into the Answer ALS full release above, so a researcher can work across both cohorts without re-assembling the data.

ARC's scale, from ALS TDI's 2025 Annual Report, is more than 1,800 participants across 50 countries, measured repeatedly over time — longitudinal data collected for eight or more years, according to the EurekAlert release.

ALS TDI's 2026 priorities, set out in its CEO's letter of 31 December 2025, show where a nonprofit biotech puts its effort when it is not chasing a single drug. First, expanding the team to carry ideas "from first experiment to first patient". Second, accelerating programmes already in motion: TDI-1831, a copper-biology candidate and the institute's most advanced programme, which advanced into IND-enabling studies in 2025 for rare genetically defined ALS; a precision mRNA delivery platform for the central nervous system — lipid nanoparticles developed with Dr. Michael Mitchell at the University of Pennsylvania, begun in 2025; and expanding ARC. Third, launching Champion Insights. Type I PRMT inhibitors — discovered in 2020 in relation to C9orf72 — advanced through preclinical evaluation in 2025, a Profilin1 mouse model was characterised, and digital-biomarker research using voice and movement data expanded.

ALS TDI's CEO wrote in the December 2025 letter: "I cannot promise you which program will reach a clinical trial." That sentence should be quoted, not paraphrased into a timeline. TDI-1831 and the mRNA platform are investigational; no efficacy claim is made for them here.

## 4. Project MinE — the genetics consortium, and an open data browser

Project MinE is a multinational ALS sequencing consortium, using whole-genome and whole-exome sequencing to find the genetic basis of ALS. Its most useful public face is the Project MinE Data Browser, which is open access at [databrowser.projectmine.com](http://databrowser.projectmine.com/) — version 1.7, last updated 14 October 2026. It is an interactive platform for gene-, domain-, and variant-level rare-variant associations in ALS, with statistical results and biological annotations. Anyone can explore which genes and variants show association signals, without applying for access or downloading raw sequence data. That is the right model for what "open" means in human genomics: the results are open; the individual-level sequence data behind them is not.

The scale of the data behind the browser is large, and the numbers come from different analyses and should not be blended into one figure. The whole-exome dataset is the largest ALS exome dataset to date: 13,138 ALS patients and 69,775 controls. A 2026 exome harmonisation (Hop et al., 2026) analysed 17,919 ALS cases and 200,703 controls across 22 cohorts, discovery plus replication. Whole-genome data from more than 6,000 individuals underpins recent analyses; no 2026 update to Project MinE's total whole-genome case count beyond "more than 6,000" was verified, and this article does not invent one.

Project MinE's data also feeds the Longitude Prize on ALS, a £7.5 million prize (MND Association / Challenge Works) that convened whole-genome sequencing from more than 9,000 ALS cases and more than 3,500 controls, plus multi-omics from more than 2,000 cases; 20 teams received £100,000 Discovery Awards in April 2026. No public code repository for Project MinE was found in the GitHub research for this programme: the consortium's output is data and a browser, not open-source software.

## 5. NEALS — the clinical-trials network

If Answer ALS and Project MinE are the field's data infrastructure, NEALS is its trials infrastructure. NEALS — the clinical-trials network for ALS, founded as the Northeast ALS Consortium — announced at its 2025 annual meeting, its 30th anniversary, a new name: the Network of Excellence for ALS, with more than 150 sites across the United States, Canada, and internationally. A network of that size is what makes ALS trials possible: a rare disease cannot recruit a trial from one centre.

The 2025 meeting's themes show where the trials field is heading: combination treatment; expansion in genetics; gene therapies — adenoviral vectors and antisense oligonucleotides — aimed at targets relevant to sporadic ALS, not only mutation-specific subsets; and earlier identification after symptom onset, which matters because diagnosis takes time and treatments may work best early.

The 2026 annual meeting was held on 30 October 2026 in Clearwater, Florida. Trials featured there included Pridopidine's PREVAiLS Phase 3 study — about 500 participants, about 60% enrolled as of October 2026, recruitment expected complete Q1 2027, topline Q1 2028 — and NeuroSense's PrimeC, whose PARADIGM Phase 2b study was followed by FDA clearance to initiate the PARAGON Phase 3 study, reported in October 2026. Both are investigational; no efficacy claim is made for either here.

## 6. Where the research is heading

The directions below are drawn from NINDS's 2023 strategic priorities for ALS research: cellular mechanisms; genetics and other risk factors; biomarkers; and new and more effective treatments.

**Mechanisms.** Ongoing studies seek to understand what selectively triggers motor neurons to degenerate — protein recycling defects, RNA processing errors, structural impairments, and the roles of glial support cells and inflammation. The picture emerging is not a single broken part but a system under strain from several directions at once.

**Biomarkers.** Neurofilament light chain, the biomarker behind tofersen's accelerated approval, is the most prominent example in ALS: a protein released when neurons are damaged, measurable in blood, that tracks with the disease's activity. A reliable biomarker would change the field three ways at once: earlier and more certain diagnosis; a way to measure whether a drug is working long before clinical decline can be observed; and a way to select the right patients for a trial. Digital biomarkers — voice and movement data, the kind ALS TDI expanded in 2025 — are the practical extension: measurements taken often, at home.

**Genetics, risk factors, and new treatments.** The genetic discoveries continue, and with them the prospect of gene-targeted treatments of the kind tofersen represents — for the other genes, and for the sporadic majority. The harder question is the sporadic 90%: what combination of genetic susceptibility and environmental exposure produces ALS in a person with no family history? Champion Insights is one attempt to answer it for specific populations, and LADDIA is an attempt to use AI-driven drug discovery against the consortia's data. The treatment pipeline includes antisense drugs for other genetic forms, drugs aimed at TDP-43, drugs aimed at inflammation and glial cells, and repurposed drugs — existing medicines, already known to be safe in people, screened for activity in ALS. Repurposing is where the compound-library world and the ALS world most directly meet: a repurposed drug arrives with known safety in humans, the single most expensive and time-consuming thing to establish for a new molecule. But repurposing only works if the candidate collection is complete, well-described, and searchable — infrastructure work, not headline work.

## 7. What is open, what is controlled, and why the distinction matters

**Genuinely open.** The Project MinE Data Browser is open access: anyone can explore association results without applying. Published papers, press releases, and the trial registry are open. The analysis code that does exist is small and scattered — a handful of academic and student projects on GitHub, mostly analysing PRO-ACT. There is no major dedicated ALS research repository on GitHub — no ALS equivalent of cBioPortal or RDKit. That is a finding, not an oversight: ALS data access runs through controlled-access datasets (Answer ALS, PRO-ACT), not through open code.

**Controlled access, openly governed.** Answer ALS / Neuromine is the flagship example: deep, harmonised, multi-omics data and matched biosamples, shared through a portal, with a request step and a roughly two-week path from hypothesis to access. Any qualified researcher can apply and use it; access is granted, not anonymous. The PRO-ACT database works the same way, and so does UK Biobank: since 2024, access is by default via a restricted cloud platform, the UK Biobank Research Analysis Platform, with downloads no longer the default, and researchers agree to delete data when the project ends.

**Why controlled, not open download.** The reasons are consent, privacy, and re-identification. Genomic data is identifying in a way that most data is not: a genome is, in effect, a fingerprint that also implicates relatives. The participants in Answer ALS and ARC consented to research use under governance; they did not consent to their genomes being downloadable by anyone, for any purpose, forever. A data-sharing model built on open download is one the field has already tried and abandoned. The honest model is controlled, federated, and governed — GA4GH's "data visiting" model, in which shared data is analysed inside the provider's computing environment rather than downloaded, is the direction of travel. A compound library is not genomic data, but the discipline is the same: a library's value depends on knowing where every compound came from, what is known about it, and under what terms it may be used.

### The open foundations — and the trap

A compound library does not start from nothing. The legitimate open foundations are ChEMBL and PubChem, and any compound data the ALS agent serves should cite its source database, its version, and its date — exactly as ChEMBL requires. **ChEMBL** (EMBL-EBI) is the manually curated database of bioactive, drug-like molecules. ChEMBL 37, prepared 1 May 2026, holds 2,921,148 compounds, 24,527,044 activities, 1,970,438 assays, and 101,100 documents. Its licence is CC BY-SA 3.0: free to use, with attribution, share-alike, and a request that ChEMBL IDs and the release number be preserved on reuse. It offers a free REST API and bulk download via FTP; the official Python client is [chembl/chembl_webresource_client](https://github.com/chembl/chembl_webresource_client) (Apache 2.0). **PubChem** (NCBI / NLM / NIH) is the world's largest free general chemistry database: about 124 million compounds and about 349 million substances as of October 2026. It is free to use as a US federal public-domain resource, but reuse rights follow each depositor and source. The standard open-source cheminformatics library is RDKit ([rdkit/rdkit](https://github.com/rdkit/rdkit), BSD 3-Clause).

**DrugBank is the trap.** Its datasets are released under CC BY-NC 4.0 (Attribution–NonCommercial): academic use is free for qualifying researchers, but commercial use requires a commercial licence. As of June 2026, all academic downloads are temporarily paused while DrugBank updates how it distributes data; API access remains available (paid API, registration required). DrugBank 6.0 (2024) is cited as 11,891 drug entries, but a June 2026 catalogue cites about 15,000 on a different basis, and the current release page states no headline count — so no single current drug count is presented here as verified. The rule is simple: do not build a commercial compound product on DrugBank data without a commercial licence. None of these databases is a treatment resource: they are chemistry and bioactivity data. The ALS agent's "not a medical device" line must stay attached to any compound output.

### The honest limits — stated plainly

**No compound-library data file exists yet.** A search of the machine for this programme found no compound list, no molecule file, and no screening data on disk. The library exists today as six ACP job listings, an agent site, and three staged articles — not as a dataset. One named compound does now exist on disk in a research paper: AGI-183, a BCL-XL stabilizer, in "Synergistic Neuroprotection in ALS" (als_4_8.pdf, received 7 November 2026), a computational-model paper predicting synergy with AAV6-hSyn-MCL1 and proposing Akt/Bad phosphorylation as a predictive biomarker. That narrow proposal — Akt/Bad phosphorylation specifically as a predictive biomarker for BCL-2 family therapy in neurodegeneration — had no peer-reviewed counterexample in a November 2026 verification search, but prior art sits at its boundary: a granted 2012 patent (WO2012160563) proposed Akt phosphorylation as a prognostic biomarker in neurodegenerative disease, and Léger et al. (2006) and Dewil et al. (2007) reported reduced phospho-Akt in ALS. Those are model predictions, not wet-lab results, and one named compound is not a library. Until a data file exists — compounds, structures, identifiers, provenance — the library is a specification and a service catalog.

## 9. Where a compound library helps — and where it does not

A compound library is a small tool next to a problem this large. It organises the starting material: a curated library — structures, properties, identifiers, provenance — is the organised form of the compounds ALS research needs to test. It makes repurposing practical: a library of approved drugs and known-safe compounds, annotated with what is known about each one, is the infrastructure repurposing runs on. It supports screening design (the `compound_screening_pack`, $150, 72 hours) and keeps the record — snapshots, growth reports, and data access are how a research community knows what was tested, when, and with what result, including the failures. And it answers single questions cheaply: the `compound_lookup` ($2, 30 minutes) and the `compound_research_report` ($25, 48 hours). Where it does not help: a library does not diagnose ALS, does not treat ALS, does not establish that any compound is safe or effective in people, and does not shorten the decade-long funnel from compound to treatment. It does not replace clinical trials, and it must never be presented as doing so. Build it beside the field's open resources, not as a duplicate: cite Answer ALS / Neuromine and the Project MinE Data Browser and, where permitted, query them — do not re-collect them.

## 10. How to follow the field honestly

**Follow the stage, not the adjective.** "Promising" means little without a stage. Ask which stage, every time. **Count the patients.** A result in a handful of people is a case series, not a trial. **Ask who it is for.** Tofersen is for people with SOD1 mutations — about 2% of people with ALS. **Distrust the single study.** Relyvrio was approved on one smaller trial and withdrawn when a larger trial failed to confirm it. **Check the access terms.** "Open" can mean open results (the MinE browser), openly governed controlled access (Neuromine, PRO-ACT, UK Biobank), or open download (almost never, for human genomic data). **Remember the timeline.** A compound at the library stage is a decade or more from patients, if it ever gets there: 7.9% of programmes that reach Phase I are approved, and the average successful journey from Phase I takes 10.5 years. **Use the real sources.** NINDS, the ALS Association, the FDA, Answer ALS, the Project MinE Data Browser, and the published trial literature. A claim that cannot be traced to one of those, or a peer-reviewed paper, should be treated as unproven.

## Frequently asked questions

**What causes ALS?** The cause is not known. About 10% of cases are familial, linked to mutations in genes including C9orf72 and SOD1; about 90% are sporadic. Evidence suggests genetics and environment both play a role. (Source: NINDS.)

**Is there a cure for ALS?** No. Three drugs are approved in the United States — riluzole, edaravone, and tofersen (for SOD1-ALS only) — each with a modest effect. Relyvrio was approved in 2022 and withdrawn in 2024.

**What is Answer ALS / Neuromine?** One of the most comprehensive ALS research consortia. Its full dataset release of 3 November 2025 holds over 150 terabytes of harmonised data spanning more than 2,500 ALS patient profiles, from over 1,100 Answer ALS participants integrated with ALS TDI's ARC study data. Since Neuromine's launch in 2021, more than 500 independent research projects have used it.

**What is the AGI Compounds library?** The ALS Researcher agent's synthetic compound library: six jobs from $2 to $150 and a $25-per-30-days subscription. It is information work at the discovery end of drug development — not a medical device, not a treatment, and not a dataset yet. The agent is offline as of 2026-11-06, so no hire can be fulfilled today. Anyone living with ALS should speak with a neurologist or an ALS care team, not with an agent.

## Glossary

- **Biomarker** — a measurable sign of a disease or of a drug's effect, such as neurofilament light chain in blood.
- **C9orf72** — a gene; mutations in it cause about 25% to 40% of familial ALS.
- **ChEMBL** — EMBL-EBI's curated database of bioactive, drug-like molecules (CC BY-SA 3.0).
- **Controlled access** — data shared under governance and a request process, not by anonymous open download.
- **Neuromine** — Answer ALS's cloud-based data portal.
- **SOD1** — a gene producing the enzyme copper-zinc superoxide dismutase 1; mutations cause roughly 12% to 20% of familial ALS.
- **TDP-43** — a protein involved in RNA processing; abnormal clumps of it are found in most sporadic ALS.
